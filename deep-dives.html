Symmetry buys conservation

expands a highlighted passage in · Newton · Frontier

momentumlagrangian mechanicsaction principle

Slide your whole laboratory three metres to the left — every magnet, every wire, every atom — and rerun the experiment. Same result. Rotate the lab, or wait a day and start again: still the same. Each of these is a continuous symmetry: a change you can make by any amount, however small, that leaves the physics untouched. Continuous is the load-bearing word. A mirror flip is all-or-nothing, but a translation can be dialed smoothly from zero — and that smooth dial is exactly what the theorem needs.

In 1918 Emmy Noether proved that every continuous symmetry of the dynamics forces a conservation law — and her proof is a machine, not an observation: feed in the symmetry and it hands you the formula for the conserved quantity. The dictionary it generates is the spine of physics. Physics unchanged under shifts in space means momentum is conserved. Unchanged under shifts in time — the experiment works the same tomorrow — means energy is conserved. Unchanged under rotation means angular momentum is conserved.

This runs deeper than Newton's third law. Action–reaction delivers momentum conservation only when forces come in tidy pairs between bodies — and they don't always. Two moving charges can push on each other in ways that fail to balance, and momentum seems to leak; it is actually flowing into the electromagnetic field. Noether's accounting catches it, because the symmetry belongs to space itself, fields included. The third law is a symptom; translation symmetry is the cause. The theorem also tells you when conservation genuinely fails: in an expanding universe, physics is not the same at every moment, and the energy of light is not conserved — photons redshift as space stretches.

One honest caveat. The theorem does not apply to any equations whatever — it needs the system written in Lagrangian form, as an action principle. Friction and drag, which forget where the energy went, have no such tidy description, so Noether is silent about them. But every fundamental theory we have — electromagnetism, general relativity, the Standard Model — is built as an action. The conservation laws you learned as rules are the shape of spacetime, transcribed.

The rocket equation from momentum flow

expands a highlighted passage in · Newton · University

momentuminertial frame

The tempting move is to write F = dp/dt for the rocket alone, with p = m(t)·v, and let the product rule loose: F = m·dv/dt + v·dm/dt. That last term is wrong — poisonously so, because it contains v, which depends on who is watching. An observer on the pad and one drifting alongside would derive different accelerations for the same rocket from the same forces, and a law that changes physics between observers is no law. F = dp/dt governs systems that don't swap matter with the outside — and a rocket alone is the opposite: a machine for hurling mass overboard.

So track everything. In a slice of time dt, the rocket — mass m, speed v — spits a parcel of exhaust dm backward at speed ve relative to the ship. With no outside force, total momentum, rocket plus exhaust, is the same before and after. Cancel terms and the bookkeeping collapses to m·dv = −ve·dm: the ship gains exactly the momentum each parcel carries away. Sum every parcel from full mass m₀ down to empty mass m₁ — an integral of dm/m — and the logarithm appears: Δv = ve ln(m₀/m₁). Tsiolkovsky wrote it down in 1903.

That logarithm is a tyrant. Reaching low-Earth orbit costs about 9.4 km/s of Δv once gravity and drag take their tax, and the best chemical engines — hydrogen burning in oxygen — exhaust at about 4.4 km/s. The ratio is 2.1, so m₀/m₁ = e2.1 ≈ 8.5: roughly 88% of what sits on the pad must be propellant, before you have paid for tanks, engines, or anything worth launching. Structure eats most of the remaining 12%.

Hence staging: the equation restarts every time you drop dead mass. Shed the empty first-stage tanks and the second stage begins its own ln(m₀/m₁) from a clean slate, no longer spending precious Δv accelerating scrap metal. The Saturn V left the pad at about 2,900 tonnes and put about 140 tonnes into orbit — under 5% — and that 5% needed three stages to exist at all.

The energy–momentum triangle

expands a highlighted passage in · E = mc² · University

momentumlorentz factorrest energy

Draw a right triangle. One leg is the rest energy, mc² — the energy an object has for merely existing. The other leg is pc, momentum times the speed of light — the motion part. The hypotenuse is total energy E, and Pythagoras is the whole law: E² = (pc)² + (mc²)². Rest and motion don't add like ordinary numbers; they add like perpendicular legs — which is why the fast regime feels so unfamiliar.

Three regimes fall out of the geometry. At rest, p = 0: the momentum leg vanishes and the hypotenuse lies along the rest leg — E = mc², the famous equation as a collapsed triangle. For light, m = 0: the rest leg vanishes and E = pc — a photon is the triangle collapsed the other way. For slow motion, expand the hypotenuse and the leading terms are E ≈ mc² + ½mv²: Newton's kinetic energy appears as the first small correction motion makes to rest energy — which is why nobody noticed the rest energy for two centuries.

Work one number. Take an electron — rest energy 511 keV — moving at 0.6c. The Lorentz factor is γ = 1/√(1 − 0.36) = 1.25, so the hypotenuse is E = γmc² ≈ 639 keV and the momentum leg is pc = γβmc² ≈ 383 keV. Check the triangle: 383² + 511² = 639², to rounding. The kinetic energy — hypotenuse minus rest leg — is 128 keV, while Newton's ½mv² predicts only 92 keV. At 60% of light speed the classical formula already runs 28% short, and it only gets worse from there.

The triangle is also the deepest statement on the page in disguise. Rearranged, (mc²)² = E² − (pc)² says mass is the frame-invariant length of the four-momentum: E and p each change between observers, but that difference of squares never does.

Mass as invariant length

expands a highlighted passage in · E = mc² · Frontier

special relativitymomentumlorentz factorinvariant mass

Relativity fuses energy and momentum into a single four-component object — the four-momentum, (E/c, p) — the way it fuses space and time into spacetime. Different observers slice it differently: the E one observer measures and the p another measures both shift with their motion, like the shadows a tilted rod casts on different walls. But one combination is identical for everyone: E² − (pc)² = (mc²)². Mass is the invariant length of the four-momentum — not any one shadow, but the rod.

This is why "relativistic mass" was retired. The old habit called γm a mass that grows with speed, but what grows with speed is energy, E = γmc²; relabelling that as mass is a unit conversion dressed up as an effect. In modern usage m is a frame-invariant label — the same number for every observer — and the Lorentz factor lives in E and p, never in m.

Invariant, though, is not additive. A system's mass is the length of its total four-momentum, and internal energy counts. A box of hot gas outweighs the same box cold: heat one kilogram of water by 100 °C and you have added 4.2×10⁵ joules, which weighs ΔE/c² ≈ 5 nanograms. Absurdly small, perfectly real — and run at the quark speeds inside a proton, the same bookkeeping becomes 99% of your body weight.

The photon is the limiting case: m = 0, so E = pc exactly — all motion, no rest. Yet take two photons of energy E flying in opposite directions: total momentum zero, total energy 2E, so the system's invariant mass is 2E/c² — mass with no massive parts anywhere in it. That is not a paradox; it is the definition working. Mass is not a stuff that things are made of. It is the frame-proof length of the energy and momentum a thing — an electron, a hot gas, a box of light — carries.

Weighing field energy

expands a highlighted passage in · E = mc² · Frontier

quarkgluonqcdinvariant mass

Put a proton on a scale and it reads 938 MeV/c². Now weigh its ingredients: two up quarks at about 2.2 MeV each, one down quark at about 4.7 MeV — call it 9 MeV of intrinsic quark mass, roughly 1% of the total. The other 99% is not stuff. It is the energy of the gluon field binding the quarks, plus the kinetic energy of the quarks rattling inside — confined energy, registering on the scale because E = mc² read right-to-left says m = E/c²: trapped energy weighs.

The trap is QCD confinement. Gluons, unlike photons, carry the very charge they mediate — color — so the field lines between quarks don't spread and dilute with distance; they bundle into a tube. Pull two quarks apart and the tube's energy grows by roughly 1 GeV for every femtometre — a tension near 1.6×10⁵ newtons, the weight of a loaded truck, acting on particles too small to measure. Long before you could separate them, the tube snaps and cashes its energy into a fresh quark–antiquark pair. No quark ever gets out alone. The energy stays — and weighs.

This is why "the Higgs gives everything mass" is mostly wrong for everyday matter. The Higgs field does set the quarks' intrinsic masses and the electron's 0.511 MeV — and that matters enormously; without electron mass there are no atoms as we know them. But switch the Higgs off in a calculation and the proton would still weigh nearly what it does. Lattice QCD — grinding the equations numerically on supercomputers — reproduces the proton's mass from confinement physics to within a few percent. Of the number on your bathroom scale, about 1% traces to the Higgs; about 99% is field energy.

One honesty note: those quark masses are not weighed in isolation — no free quark has ever been observed — so the values are extracted indirectly and depend on the theoretical convention used to define them. The 99% headline is robust; the fine print of the 1% is convention.

What ψ actually is

expands a highlighted passage in · Schrödinger · High school

wavefunctionborn ruleinterferencesuperposition

ψ is not a probability. It is a probability amplitude — at every point in space, a complex number, best pictured as a small arrow with a length and a direction (the phase). The Born rule says probability is the arrow's length, squared. The direction never shows up in any single measurement — no detector reads out phase — and yet the direction decides everything, because of what happens when arrows meet.

Here is the quantum rule of combination. When an event can happen two ways you cannot even in principle tell apart, you do not add the probabilities. You add the arrows — tip to tail, directions included — and square the result. Two aligned arrows of equal length give four times the single-path probability, not two. Two opposed arrows give zero: an event, each of whose routes is perfectly possible, that cannot happen at all. That add-then-square structure is interference, and it is the entire difference between quantum mechanics and coin-flipping.

The double slit is this arithmetic made visible. At each point on the screen the electron's amplitude arrives by two routes, one per slit, and each arrow's direction turns steadily along its path — a longer path means more turn. Where the two arrive aligned: bright stripe. Where the path difference has rotated one arrow to point against the other: darkness — an electron forbidden to land where either slit alone would happily deliver it.

Now the question that opens the door: where does ψ live? For one particle, an arrow at each point of ordinary space. For two particles, an arrow for each pair of positions — ψ(x₁, x₂), a wave over configuration space: six dimensions for two particles, 3N for N. A wave over pairs need not split into one story per particle, and that irreducibility is entanglement. Whether the arrows are physically real or a perfect bookkeeping of what can be known remains the honest open question. The arithmetic, meanwhile, has never missed.

Why sharper x means fuzzier p

expands a highlighted passage in · Schrödinger · University

momentumuncertainty principlesuperposition

Start from a rule older than the equation itself: de Broglie's p = h/λ. A quantum particle's momentum is set by its wavelength — shorter wave, harder punch. So a wave with one perfectly pure wavelength, crest after identical crest forever, has one exact momentum. Now look at what that purity costs. The wave extends unchanged from horizon to horizon; every location on it looks like every other. Ask where the particle is and there is no answer. Exact momentum has purchased total ignorance of position.

To localize the particle you must build a lump — a wave packet — and the only way to build a lump out of waves is to add many different wavelengths, phased so they reinforce in one spot and cancel everywhere else. The narrower the lump, the more wavelengths the recipe demands. And since each wavelength is a momentum, a sharp position is literally built from a spread of momenta. This trade-off is a theorem about waves — the Fourier bandwidth limit — with nothing quantum in it: a held flute note has definite pitch, a sharp click contains every frequency, and no engineer can make a signal both brief and pure.

Quantum mechanics adds exactly one ingredient — matter is the wave, and momentum is its wavelength — and the trade-off becomes physics: Δx·Δp ≥ ħ/2, with ħ ≈ 1.05×10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds. In the formal theory the same fact is compressed into [x̂, p̂] = iħ — position and momentum operators fail to commute, which is algebra's way of saying no state can be sharp in both at once. The uncertainty principle is not measurement clumsiness; it is the geometry of waves, inherited by matter.

Put a number on it. Confine an electron to an atom's width — Δx ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m, one ångström. Then Δp ≥ ħ/(2Δx) ≈ 5×10⁻²⁵ kg·m/s, which for the electron's 9.1×10⁻³¹ kg mass is a velocity spread near 580 km/s and a kinetic energy of order one electron-volt; shrink the box toward hydrogen's actual half-ångström and the estimate climbs toward the 13.6 eV that binds the atom. That is why atoms have the size they do: squeeze an electron tighter and its momentum spread — hence its kinetic energy — rises faster than the nucleus's pull can pay.

How the classical world emerges

expands a highlighted passage in · Schrödinger · Frontier

entanglementborn ruleinterferencedecoherence

The Schrödinger equation is linear, so it happily writes superpositions of anything — an electron through two slits, and in principle a cat in two conditions. The question is not why superpositions exist but why you never see one. Seeing a superposition means seeing interference: the branches carry phases, and the observable signature — fringes, beats — lives in the cross terms where one branch meets the other. Kill the cross terms and a superposition becomes, for every practical purpose, an ordinary either/or.

Decoherence is what kills them. A macroscopic object cannot keep its branches private: every air molecule that bounces off it, every photon that scatters from it, flies away carrying which-branch information — the object becomes entangled with its environment. Interference requires bringing the branches back together coherently, and once trillions of escaping particles each hold a record of which branch they met, no measurement on the object alone can recover the cross terms. The branches don't vanish; their ability to interfere does — leaked, irreversibly for all practical purposes, into the environment.

The timescales are what make this a law of daily life rather than a technicality. Standard estimates going back to Joos and Zeh (1985): for a 10-micrometre dust grain superposed across its own width, scattering air molecules at atmospheric pressure destroys coherence in roughly 10⁻³¹ seconds; the best laboratory vacuum still manages about 10⁻¹⁴ s; even in deep space, cosmic microwave background photons alone finish the job in about a second. This is why cats never visibly superpose, and why quantum computers demand heroic isolation and cold — engineering a machine is a fight to slow decoherence down.

Now the honest limit. Decoherence explains why you will never observe interference between "alive" and "dead." It does not say which one happens. After decoherence, the mathematics still describes multiple branches, weighted by Born-rule probabilities; selecting a single outcome — or explaining why experience contains only one — is the measurement problem, and it remains open. Collapse theories, many-worlds, and pilot-wave mechanics each answer it differently. Decoherence is the part they all share; it is not, by itself, the answer.

Resonance as a pole

expands a highlighted passage in · Resonance · Frontier

q factorbreit wignerinvariant mass

Let frequency go complex. A struck bell doesn't just oscillate — it oscillates and dies away, and one complex number captures both: write the motion as e−iωt with ω = ω₀ − iΓ/2, and the real part ω₀ sets the ring frequency while the imaginary part sets the decay — amplitude falling as e−Γt/2, stored energy as e−Γt. A ringing, fading tone is a single point in the complex frequency plane. The lifetime is τ = 1/Γ, and the quality factor Q = ω₀/Γ counts, roughly, how many times the system swings before the ring dies.

Now drive the system and watch its response as you sweep a real frequency. The response function is largest where you pass closest to that complex point — the pole. You can never reach it (that would need a complex drive), but skimming past it on the real axis traces out a bump: peak at ω₀, full width Γ at half maximum. That curve is the Lorentzian — in particle physics, the Breit–Wigner. And here is the universality: any linear system whose response has an isolated pole produces the same shape. That is why one formula fits a bell, an atom absorbing light, a tuned radio circuit, and a bump in collider data — they differ only in where the pole sits.

In quantum theory energy is frequency times ħ, so a short-lived particle is a pole at complex energy: the real part is its mass, the imaginary part its width Γ, and its lifetime is τ = ħ/Γ, with ħ = 6.58×10⁻¹⁶ eV·s. The Higgs boson, mass 125 GeV, has a predicted width of about 4.1 MeV — so τ = 6.58×10⁻¹⁶ eV·s ÷ 4.1×10⁶ eV ≈ 1.6×10⁻²² s. The bump in an invariant-mass spectrum is the particle's death announcement, and its width is a stopwatch.

One honest footnote: the Higgs bump you see in LHC plots is roughly 1–2 GeV wide — hundreds of times the true 4 MeV — because that is the detectors' energy resolution, not the particle's width. The width is instead inferred indirectly, from how often the Higgs is produced far off its resonance peak; current measurements are consistent with the ~4 MeV prediction but still carry MeV-scale error bars. The pole picture spans an absurd range: from 10⁻²² s particle resonances to optical clock lines with Q beyond 10¹⁶ — one mathematics, twenty orders of magnitude apart.

The Gamow window, with numbers

expands a highlighted passage in · The Sun ensemble

tunnelingfusioncross section

First, the impasse in numbers. The Sun's core runs at 15.7 million kelvin, which sounds like an inferno but converts to a typical thermal energy of kT ≈ 1.35 keV per particle. To touch and fuse, two protons must climb their mutual electric repulsion — the Coulomb barrier — which at nuclear range, around a femtometre, stands near 1 MeV. That is roughly 700 times the thermal energy on offer. Classically, the fraction of protons carrying barrier-topping energy is the Boltzmann factor e−E/kT ≈ e−700, about 10⁻³²⁰. The Sun holds some 10⁵⁷ protons; classically, not one of them clears the wall. This is the precise sense in which the core is "a thousand times too cold."

Tunneling rewrites the ledger. A proton wave doesn't need to clear the barrier — a sliver leaks through, with probability scaling as e−√(E_G/E), where the Gamow energy E_G ≈ 493 keV for two protons. That exponential rises ferociously with collision energy: at the thermal 1.35 keV the leak-through factor is about e−19 ≈ 5×10⁻⁹, while at 6 keV it is about e−9 ≈ 10⁻⁴ — twenty-thousand-fold better odds for four times the energy.

So two curves fight. The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution says fast protons are exponentially rare; tunneling says fast protons are exponentially lucky. Multiply a steeply falling curve by a steeply rising one and you get a narrow spike where their product peaks — the Gamow window. For the Sun's proton–proton step it sits near 6 keV, several times the typical thermal energy, and only a few keV wide. Nearly all solar fusion is performed by the small aristocracy of protons passing through that band — energetic enough to tunnel, still common enough to matter.

The window's steepness is destiny for stars. Because both exponentials are sharp, the fusion rate behaves near solar conditions like T⁴ for the proton–proton chain — and like roughly T¹⁶ or steeper for the CNO cycle that dominates in heavier stars. Small differences in core temperature become enormous differences in output: that is why a star modestly more massive than the Sun burns furiously and dies young, and why the Sun's own thermostat — swell a little, cool a little, throttle back — grips so firmly. The whole hierarchy of stellar lifetimes hangs on the shape of one narrow window.

The neutron's gauntlet, quantified

expands a highlighted passage in · Reactor ensemble

resonance escape probabilitymoderatorcriticalitycross section

Follow one neutron. It is born in fission at about 2 MeV, moving near a tenth of light speed — and it is nearly useless at that energy, because fast neutrons barely split uranium-235. To join the chain it must slow to thermal energy, 0.025 eV: an energy drop of a factor of nearly 100 million. In a water-moderated reactor that takes about 18–20 elastic collisions with hydrogen nuclei, each collision shaving off a chunk of energy. The descent is a staircase, not a slide — and partway down, the staircase passes through a minefield.

The mines are uranium-238's capture resonances: razor-thin energy bands, mostly between a few eV and a few keV, where the nucleus's appetite for neutrons explodes. At the first and largest, 6.7 eV, the capture cross-section leaps to several thousand barns — against a few barns between resonances, a thousandfold spike. A slowing neutron hops down in discrete energy steps; land a hop inside a resonance while near U-238, and the neutron is swallowed — lost to the chain. The fraction that survives the sprint is the resonance escape probability, p — one of the four numbers in k = ηεpf, the four-factor formula that decides criticality. In typical thermal reactors p ≈ 0.7–0.8: even in a well-designed core, roughly one slowing neutron in four is eaten mid-descent.

Geometry is what makes p that high. Blend natural uranium uniformly through a moderator and every neutron does its slowing with U-238 constantly in reach — p collapses, and the mixture can never go critical. Fermi and Szilárd's 1942 fix was to lump the fuel: in rods, neutrons wander out into pure moderator, do their dangerous slowing there, and return to the fuel already below the resonance energies. Lumping adds a second gift, self-shielding — at 6.7 eV absorption is so fierce that the rod's outer skin soaks up nearly all resonance-energy arrivals, leaving the interior's U-238 effectively out of the game and raising p further.

The same physics is the reactor's built-in brake. Heat the fuel and its U-238 nuclei jitter faster, Doppler-broadening the resonances into wider traps: p drops, k drops, the chain throttles back — a negative feedback written into nuclear structure, no software required.

The 38-microsecond budget

expands a highlighted passage in · GPS ensemble

general relativityspecial relativityhyperfine splitting

Both numbers in the famous budget are one-line estimates you can check. Start with gravity. General relativity says a clock higher in a gravity well ticks faster, and for weak fields the fractional rate change is simply ΔΦ/c² — the difference in gravitational potential (energy per kilogram of altitude gained) divided by the speed of light squared. Earth's GM is 3.99×10¹⁴ m³/s²; a GPS satellite orbits at radius 26,571 km (20,200 km of altitude above Earth's 6,371 km radius). The potential difference works out to ΔΦ ≈ 4.8×10⁷ J/kg, so ΔΦ/c² ≈ 5.3×10⁻¹⁰. Multiply by 86,400 seconds in a day: the orbiting clock runs about 45.7 microseconds per day fast. That is the +45.

Now speed. Special relativity slows a moving clock by the Lorentz factor, which for slow speeds expands to a fractional lag of v²/2c². Orbital velocity at that radius is 3.9 km/s, so v²/2c² ≈ 8.4×10⁻¹¹ — times 86,400 s, about 7.2 μs per day slow. That is the −7. Gravity wins the tug-of-war by a landslide: net, the satellite clock gains ≈ 38.6 μs per day on a ground clock.

Why such fuss over microseconds? Because GPS is a ranging system: your receiver multiplies signal travel times by the speed of light, and light covers about 300 metres per microsecond. An uncorrected 38.6 μs of daily clock error is roughly 11.6 km of accumulated ranging error per day — the blue dot walks off the map before lunch. So the correction is baked in at the factory: the required fractional slowdown, 38.6 μs ÷ 86,400 s ≈ 4.47×10⁻¹⁰, is subtracted from the clock's base frequency. Instead of 10.23 MHz, each clock is set to 10.22999999543 MHz — about 4.6 millihertz low — so that, observed from the ground, it ticks 10.23 MHz exactly once in orbit.

Two honest footnotes. These are leading-order estimates: the real bookkeeping compares the satellite against a clock on Earth's rotating geoid, where the ground clock's own motion and potential are already folded in — but the first-order arithmetic above lands within a fraction of a microsecond of the engineered number. And the 38.6 μs is only the constant part: because the orbits are slightly elliptical, each satellite's clock speeds and slows once per revolution, a residual your receiver corrects satellite-by-satellite, tens of nanoseconds at a time.
